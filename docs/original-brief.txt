Act as a senior software product manager, geospatial software architect, remote-sensing scientist, and machine-learning engineer.

Create a comprehensive, implementation-ready **Functional Requirements Document (FRD)** for software that detects and maps visible mining locations from satellite imagery.

Write the actual FRD, not just an outline. Explain technical concepts clearly enough for a founder to understand while providing sufficient detail for a development team to estimate and build the product.

## 1. Project context and objective

I have experience in geoinformatics, remote sensing, geophysics, geology, mineral mining, and mining business. I want to establish a technology company, initially focusing on Nigeria and later expanding across Africa.

The software should identify candidate surface-mining footprints, map their boundaries, and eventually monitor changes over time.

My longer-term business ambition is to connect African mineral supply with European buyers through better information, verification, and traceability. However, the first software release must focus on mining-location detection.

Clearly distinguish:

- Detection of visible mining-related land disturbance.
- Detection of changes in mining footprints.
- Identification of mineral types.
- Prediction of undiscovered mineral deposits.
- Assessment of legal status or ownership.

Only the first capability belongs in the core MVP. Change detection may be a subsequent phase. The remaining capabilities must not be implied by mining-footprint detection.

Do not promise perfect performance. Define measurable reliability, uncertainty, known limitations, and circumstances where the system should decline to produce a confident result.

## 2. Candidate datasets

Evaluate these datasets as candidates, verifying their current documentation, availability, contents, licences, and underlying imagery restrictions:

1. MineSegSAT:
   https://zenodo.org/records/12667829
   https://github.com/macdonaldezra/MineSegSAT

2. Global ML-ready dataset for mining areas:
   https://zenodo.org/records/14195737

3. DRC artisanal and small-scale mining dataset:
   https://zenodo.org/records/15257800

4. Global-scale mining polygons, Maus et al., Version 2:
   https://doi.pangaea.de/10.1594/PANGAEA.942325

5. MineNetCD:
   https://github.com/AI4RS/MineNetCD

6. A future locally verified Nigerian dataset collected through field observations, suitable reference imagery, and operator partnerships.

Do not assume all these datasets can be pooled, trained sequentially, or used commercially.

Provide a dataset compatibility matrix covering:

- Task and label definition.
- Geographic coverage.
- Sensor, bands, resolution, and observation dates.
- Imagery included versus imagery that must be retrieved separately.
- Segmentation masks, bounding boxes, or change labels.
- Licence and commercial-use status.
- Preprocessing requirements.
- Expected relevance to Nigeria.
- Known duplication or overlapping source labels.
- Recommended role: baseline, training, validation, external testing, or separate research branch.

Flag unverified information explicitly. Cite primary sources for externally verified claims.

## 3. Training and evaluation strategy

My initial idea is to train on the datasets one after another. Evaluate that idea critically.

Compare:

- Training on one suitable dataset as a baseline.
- Sequential fine-tuning.
- Combined or balanced multi-dataset training.
- Transfer learning from pretrained models.
- Fine-tuning on Nigerian examples.
- Separate models for different sensors or tasks where necessary.

Address catastrophic forgetting, domain shift, conflicting label definitions, class imbalance, and differences between industrial and artisanal mining.

Recommend an experiment sequence with clear decision gates. Keep training orchestration separate from the customer-facing detection workflow.

Require:

- A simple baseline and at least one suitable segmentation approach.
- Consistent preprocessing between training and inference.
- Geographic/site-level separation of training, validation, and test data.
- Temporal separation when testing change-monitoring claims.
- Duplicate and overlapping-scene detection across datasets.
- Training-only fitting of normalisation parameters.
- Independent Nigerian testing.
- Reproducible experiment records.
- Dataset, code, configuration, and model versioning.
- Comparison against previous models before promotion.
- Rollback when a new model performs worse.

Do not equate a model score with a calibrated probability unless calibration has been evaluated.

## 4. Users and workflows

Define appropriate roles, including:

- Administrator.
- ML/data administrator.
- GIS analyst.
- Field reviewer.
- Customer or read-only viewer.

Describe end-to-end workflows for:

1. Creating a project.
2. Selecting an area of interest by map, coordinates, or uploaded boundary.
3. Selecting observation dates.
4. Retrieving supported imagery or uploading compatible imagery.
5. Checking imagery quality and coverage.
6. Running detection.
7. Viewing candidate footprints, scores, and evidence.
8. Confirming, rejecting, or editing detections.
9. Exporting results and reports.
10. Adding reviewed examples to a controlled annotation pool.
11. Preparing, training, evaluating, and approving a new model.
12. Comparing observations across dates in a later release.

Human-reviewed predictions must not automatically become ground truth without quality control.

## 5. Functional requirements

Assign every requirement a unique ID, priority, user role, dependencies, and testable acceptance criteria.

Cover:

- Authentication and role-based access.
- Project and area-of-interest management.
- Imagery discovery, ingestion, and metadata tracking.
- Coordinate-system handling.
- Band, resolution, and input-schema validation.
- Cloud, shadow, no-data, and usable-coverage handling.
- Tiling, mosaicking, and large-area processing.
- Training-data ingestion and validation.
- Annotation and review tools.
- Dataset split management.
- Training configuration and experiment tracking.
- Model registry and promotion approval.
- Batch inference and asynchronous job management.
- Mining-footprint segmentation.
- Raster-to-vector conversion and configurable minimum mapping unit.
- Confidence display and uncertain-result handling.
- Map visualisation and supporting imagery.
- Human review and audit history.
- Search and filtering.
- Export to GeoJSON, GeoPackage, GeoTIFF, and CSV where appropriate.
- Report generation.
- Error handling, retries, cancellation, and resumable jobs.
- Usage and processing-cost visibility.
- Optional future change detection.

Distinguish “no mining detected” from “insufficient usable imagery.”

For each exported detection, specify appropriate fields such as project ID, detection ID, geometry, area, imagery dates, sensor, model version, score, review status, reviewer evidence, and processing timestamp.

## 6. Evaluation and acceptance

Specify evaluation at both pixel and site/object levels.

Include:

- Precision.
- Recall.
- F1 score.
- Intersection over Union.
- Site-level detection performance.
- False alerts per unit area.
- Boundary or area-estimation quality where relevant.
- Performance by geography, site size, mining type, season, and imagery quality.
- Runtime and processing cost per unit area.
- Uncertainty in reported metrics.

Avoid overall accuracy as the primary success measure.

Propose provisional performance targets for a pilot, but label them as hypotheses requiring baseline experiments and customer validation. Do not invent achieved results.

Define:

- Minimum usable input conditions.
- Pilot acceptance criteria.
- Model release criteria.
- Reasons to reject or flag results.
- External field-validation procedures.
- Evaluation of false negatives as well as false positives.

## 7. Architecture and non-functional requirements

Recommend a practical architecture while distinguishing product requirements from implementation choices.

Consider:

- Web-based map interface.
- Geospatial backend/API.
- Spatial database.
- Imagery and model object storage.
- Background job queue.
- Separate training and inference workers.
- GPU access where justified.
- Experiment tracking and model registry.
- Monitoring and audit logging.

Assess a lean open-source stack, explaining trade-offs without overengineering the MVP.

Address:

- Limited internet connectivity and resumable uploads.
- Security and sensitive site locations.
- Customer data isolation and access controls.
- Backup and recovery.
- Scalability.
- Maintainability.
- Observability.
- Storage lifecycle and cost management.
- External data-service failures.
- Commercial imagery and dataset permissions.
- Hardware options for development, training, and deployment.

Do not assume free imagery means free cloud processing or unrestricted access through every platform.

## 8. Screens, data model, and interfaces

Provide:

- A screen inventory and the purpose of each screen.
- Main entities and relationships.
- Required metadata and validation rules.
- API capability outline.
- Job states and failure states.
- Model and dataset lifecycle states.

Include compact diagrams only where they clarify the architecture or workflow.

## 9. Delivery scope and roadmap

Separate:

- MVP.
- Nigerian pilot.
- Commercial release.
- Future research and expansion.

Prioritise one sensor-compatible, single-date mining-footprint detection workflow before adding multiple sensors, change detection, or mineral prospectivity.

For each phase, specify deliverables, dependencies, validation gates, and major risks.

Provide an implementation backlog grouped into epics and user stories, with acceptance criteria.

Estimate effort and costs as explicit ranges with assumptions. Separate data preparation, annotation, engineering, model development, field validation, and operating expenses. Avoid unsupported precision.

## 10. Required final structure

Deliver the FRD with:

1. Executive summary.
2. Product purpose and measurable objectives.
3. Assumptions and unresolved decisions.
4. Scope and exclusions.
5. Users and workflows.
6. Dataset assessment and compatibility matrix.
7. Functional requirements.
8. ML training, evaluation, and release requirements.
9. Data model and interfaces.
10. Screens and map interactions.
11. Architecture and non-functional requirements.
12. Acceptance and field-validation plan.
13. Delivery phases and prioritised backlog.
14. Risks, dependencies, and cost assumptions.
15. Recommended first development milestone.
16. Source references.

Proceed with reasonable, explicitly stated assumptions rather than stopping for a long questionnaire. Finish with no more than five questions whose answers would materially change the design.

The document must make it clear what developers should build, what scientists must validate, and what evidence is required before the software can be sold as reliable.
